Methodology

Fully transparent, formula-based prioritization. No machine learning. No synthetic data. Every number traced back to its public source.

✓
What This Is

  • A data-driven prioritization framework ranking Miami-Dade traffic signals by estimated disruption impact
  • Signals scored by how much traffic disruption they would cause if they went dark during a storm
  • Two real, public data components: traffic volume (AADT 2025) and historical hurricane proximity (HURDAT2 1851–2023)
  • Percentile-based tier assignment (HIGH / MODERATE / LOW) computed from the priority scores
  • All 1,572 signals are real FDOT inventory locations, queried April 2026

✗
What This Is NOT

  • Not a prediction of which signals will lose power — no public outage records exist
  • Not a machine learning model — no training data, no learned weights, no AUC metric
  • Not synthetic data — every number is from a verified public dataset
  • Not a claim that higher-ranked signals will fail — this is a preparedness planning tool
  • Not using utility outage data — FPL does not report SAIDI/SAIFI in EIA Form 861 (2022)

Step 1
AADT Score — Traffic Volume Normalization
Source: FDOT ArcGIS REST API · Annual Average Daily Traffic 2025 · 1,848 road segments joined to 1,572 signals
Formula
AADT_score = (signal_AADT − min_AADT) / (max_AADT − min_AADT)
Dataset range: min_AADT = 1,500 veh/day · max_AADT = 271,000 veh/day · denominator = 271,000 − 1,500 = 269,500
Why min-max normalization? AADT values span 1,500 to 271,000 vehicles/day — a 180× range. Min-max scaling maps every signal to [0, 1] so AADT and hurricane scores contribute equally to the final weighted sum. A signal at the dataset maximum gets AADT_score = 1.000; a signal at the minimum gets 0.000. The formula is applied independently to each of the 1,572 signals.
Example A
Signal #3833 — Coastal / Hurricane-Close
Given: AADT = 39,500 veh/day
AADT_score = (39,500 − 1,500) / (271,000 − 1,500)
AADT_score = 38,000 / 269,500
AADT_score = 0.141
Signal #3833 handles moderate traffic — 39,500 veh/day. A score of 0.141 means it sits in the lower 14% of the dataset by traffic volume alone. Its high overall priority will come from hurricane proximity (Step 2).
Example B
Signal #4110 — High-Volume Arterial
Given: AADT = 271,000 veh/day
AADT_score = (271,000 − 1,500) / (271,000 − 1,500)
AADT_score = 269,500 / 269,500
AADT_score = 1.000
Signal #4110 sits on Miami-Dade's highest-volume road segment — 271,000 veh/day. It gets the maximum AADT score of 1.000. Losing this signal during a storm would disrupt more drivers than any other signal in the dataset.
Step 2
Hurricane Proximity Score — Storm Exposure Normalization
Source: NOAA HURDAT2 Atlantic Hurricane Database 1851–2023 · 54,749 track points from 1,973 named storms · Haversine distance computed for all signal–track combinations
Formula (two sub-steps)
raw_exposure = 1 / closest_storm_km    ← invert: closer storm = higher exposure value
hurr_score = (raw_exposure − min_exposure) / (max_exposure − min_exposure)
Dataset range: closest_storm_km spans 0.15 km (most exposed) → 12.24 km (least exposed) across all 1,572 signals
→ max_exposure = 1/0.15 = 6.667  ·  min_exposure = 1/12.24 = 0.0817  ·  denominator = 6.667 − 0.0817 = 6.585
Why invert the distance? Storm proximity is naturally a distance — smaller = more exposed. By computing 1/distance, we convert it to an exposure measure where larger = more exposed. This is then min-max normalized to [0, 1].

Why closest track distance, not storm count? Within Miami-Dade's 5,000 km² area, every signal has 198–211 storms within 250 km — an extremely narrow range with no spatial variation. Storm count would rank all signals nearly identically. Closest track distance gives real differentiation: coastal signals had a storm center pass within 0.15 km; inland signals show 12+ km minimum over 172 years of records.
Example A
Signal #3833 — Coastal (25.899°N, 80.300°W)
Given: closest_storm_km = 0.15 km
raw_exposure = 1 / 0.15 = 6.667
hurr_score = (6.667 − 0.0817) / (6.667 − 0.0817)
hurr_score = 6.585 / 6.585
hurr_score = 1.000
In 172 years of HURDAT2 records, a hurricane center passed within 150 meters of Signal #3833. It holds the maximum hurricane score in the entire dataset. This is a coastal southern Miami-Dade location directly in the historical storm corridor.
Example B
Signal #4110 — Inland (25.931°N, 80.209°W)
Given: closest_storm_km = 9.73 km
raw_exposure = 1 / 9.73 = 0.1028
hurr_score = (0.1028 − 0.0817) / (6.667 − 0.0817)
hurr_score = 0.0211 / 6.585
hurr_score = 0.003
Signal #4110 is inland Miami-Dade. The closest storm center in all 1,973 HURDAT2 storms was still 9.73 km away. It gets a near-zero hurricane score. Its final priority rank will come entirely from its maximum AADT.
Step 3
Final Priority Score — Equal-Weighted Combination
Combines both normalized components with equal 50/50 weight · Output range: [0, 1]
Final Formula
Priority_Score = 0.5 × AADT_score + 0.5 × hurr_score
Equal weighting applied because no historical power outage records exist to empirically calibrate the weights. 50/50 is the most defensible, transparent choice under data constraints.
Why equal weighting? A higher weight on AADT would rank signals purely by traffic volume, ignoring storm geography. A higher weight on hurricane exposure would favor coastal signals even with negligible traffic. Without ground-truth outage data to fit the weights empirically, 0.5/0.5 avoids making an unverifiable assumption about which factor matters more.
Example A
Signal #3833 — Final Score
AADT_score = 0.141  ·  hurr_score = 1.000
Priority = 0.5 × 0.141 + 0.5 × 1.000
Priority = 0.0705 + 0.5000
Priority Score = 0.5705  →  HIGH (Rank #1 of 1,572)
Despite modest traffic (only 14% of the AADT range), Signal #3833 ranks first across all 1,572 signals because a hurricane center has passed within 150 m of it. Storm exposure completely dominates the score. This is the correct outcome — this location has the highest historical hurricane exposure in the county.
Example B
Signal #4110 — Final Score
AADT_score = 1.000  ·  hurr_score = 0.003
Priority = 0.5 × 1.000 + 0.5 × 0.003
Priority = 0.5000 + 0.0015
Priority Score = 0.5016  →  HIGH (Rank #2 of 1,572)
Signal #4110 has the highest AADT in the entire dataset — 271,000 veh/day — and that traffic volume alone is enough to place it second. This shows how two completely different pathways (storm exposure vs. traffic volume) can both produce HIGH-priority results through different mechanisms.

What Each Tier Means in Practice
HIGH Priority
Top 25% — 393 Signals

These signals are on the highest-volume roads, the most storm-exposed corridors, or both at significant levels. If they go dark during a storm:

  • Maximum vehicle throughput disrupted — gridlock on major arterials and evacuation routes
  • Emergency response times increase — officers dispatched to manually direct traffic at dark intersections
  • Cascading queue spillback onto adjacent signals as vehicles back up
  • Coastal HIGH signals face direct hurricane wind, surge, and immediate post-landfall outage risk
→ First priority for backup power assessment, pre-storm inspection, and fastest restoration timeline
MODERATE Priority
25th–75th Percentile — 787 Signals

These signals carry meaningful traffic or sit in a moderately storm-exposed area — but not both at the maximum level:

  • Medium-volume roads: outage causes delays but not complete gridlock on major corridors
  • Storm exposure is real but the closest historical storm center was 2–8 km away
  • Disruption is significant at neighborhood or secondary corridor level
  • After HIGH signals are addressed, these are the next concern
→ Second-wave inspection priority; include in backup power planning if resources allow
LOW Priority
Bottom 25% — 392 Signals

These signals have low traffic volume AND are historically far from storm centers — both conditions together:

  • Low-volume local roads: outage causes minor delays, easier for drivers to navigate unsignalized
  • Closest historical storm center was 8–12+ km away across all 1,973 HURDAT2 storms
  • Lower consequential impact means allocating resources here last is defensible
  • Still real signals that will need restoration — but not first
→ Lowest urgency for storm prep — restore after HIGH and MODERATE are cleared
Key Design Decisions

Why HURDAT2 Closest Track Distance (Not Storm Count)

Within Miami-Dade's 5,000 km² area, every signal has 198–211 storms within 250 km — an extremely narrow range that provides no meaningful spatial variation. Using storm count would rank all signals nearly identically. Instead, closest storm track distance gives real spatial differentiation: coastal signals have had storm centers pass within 0.15 km, while inland signals show 12+ km minimum distances over 172 years of records.

Why EIA Utility Reliability Data Was Excluded

Florida Power & Light (FPL), which serves Miami-Dade, does not report SAIDI or SAIFI in EIA Form 861 (2022). EIA Form 861 was reviewed — 34 Florida utilities are present, but FPL's reliability columns are entirely blank. Including an empty field as a scoring input would be misleading and non-transparent. This is documented in the Data Sources tab and the output CSV.
